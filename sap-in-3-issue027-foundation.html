There is a pattern that repeats across almost every enterprise AI program, and once you have seen it a few times it becomes impossible to miss. An organization identifies a promising use case. It runs a pilot. The pilot works, often impressively, in the controlled conditions of a proof of concept. Enthusiasm builds. And then the program tries to move from pilot to production, and something goes wrong that nobody quite anticipated. The data the model needs turns out to live in four systems that do not agree with each other. The process the AI is meant to automate turns out to be a thicket of custom modifications that no one fully documented. The outputs cannot be trusted because the underlying data cannot be traced. The pilot that worked in a demo cannot survive contact with the real operating environment. And the program quietly joins the large majority that never reaches production at all.

The instinct, when this happens, is to look at the AI. A better model, a different vendor, a more sophisticated approach. That instinct is almost always wrong. The thing that failed was not above the waterline, where the models live. It was below it, in the foundation of data and process and governance that the model was standing on. This issue of SAP in 3 is about that foundation. It covers why the foundation, not the model, is the real constraint on enterprise AI; how SAP builds that foundation through two specific investments, Clean Core and SAP Business Data Cloud; and what changes for an organization when the ground beneath its AI is finally strong enough to carry the weight it is being asked to bear.

Finding One — Why
01
Why the Foundation, Not the Model, Is the Real Constraint on Enterprise AI

The most useful mental model for understanding enterprise AI is a waterline. Above the waterline sit the large language models and the general-purpose AI capabilities that dominate the public conversation. They improve constantly, they are trained on the vast body of publicly available information, and they are genuinely remarkable at tasks that draw on broad knowledge and language. Below the waterline sits everything that makes an enterprise specific: its own data, its own processes, its own compliance obligations, its own history of how work actually gets done. The models above the waterline are improving at a pace no single organization can match. The foundation below the waterline is where almost every organization is actually stuck.

This matters because AI does not create capability out of nothing. It operates on the foundation it is given. An AI deployed on fragmented, ungoverned, inconsistent data does not transcend those problems through sheer intelligence. It inherits them, and because it operates at machine speed and scale, it propagates them faster than any human process ever could. The organizations that struggle with AI are rarely struggling because the models are inadequate. They are struggling because the foundation beneath the models was built for a different era, one where data lived in silos because no one needed it unified, where the core was customized because flexibility mattered more than upgradability, and where governance was a periodic audit rather than a continuous requirement. Those were reasonable choices at the time. They are now the single largest barrier between an AI ambition and an AI in production.

Above the Waterline — Improving on Its Own
The large language models and general-purpose AI capabilities. Trained on public information, improving at a pace no single enterprise can match, and increasingly a commodity available to everyone. This is not where competitive advantage is won, because everyone has access to the same rising tide.

Below the Waterline — Where Organizations Are Stuck
Your data, your processes, your governance, your institutional knowledge. This is the foundation the AI actually operates on, it is specific to your organization, and it is where the real work of making AI succeed lives. Competitive advantage in enterprise AI is won here, below the waterline, not above it.
The Three Foundation Failures That Stall AI Before Production
🧩
Fragmented Data Without a Common Meaning
The data an AI needs is spread across multiple systems that define the same thing in different ways. A customer in one system is not the same record as the customer in another. Finance, operations, and the data warehouse each hold a version of the truth, and none of them fully agree. An AI operating on this foundation cannot produce reliable answers, because the question it is answering does not have a single, consistent set of facts underneath it. No model is intelligent enough to reconcile a data estate that was never designed to agree with itself.
🔧
A Customized Core That Cannot Be Trusted or Upgraded
Years of custom modifications to the digital core produce a system that behaves in ways no one fully documented and that cannot be upgraded without risk. An AI agent trying to act within that system encounters logic that deviates from the standard in undocumented ways. The modifications that once provided flexibility now prevent the organization from adopting the very capabilities that flexibility was meant to enable. A heavily modified core is not an asset for AI. It is an obstacle.
🔍
Governance and Lineage That Do Not Exist Yet
In a regulated industry, an AI output that cannot be traced to its source data is an output that cannot be used. If no one can say which data produced a recommendation, under what controls, and with what lineage, then the recommendation fails the first question a regulator or auditor will ask. Governance is not a constraint that slows AI down. In the enterprise, it is the precondition that makes AI usable at all. An organization that has not built it will discover the gap at the worst possible moment.

Finding Two — How
02
How SAP Builds the Foundation — Clean Core and SAP Business Data Cloud

SAP's answer to the foundation problem rests on two investments that address the two halves of it. The first is Clean Core, which addresses the process and system half: keeping the digital core standard, governable, and upgradable so that AI can operate within it reliably. The second is SAP Business Data Cloud, which addresses the data half: unifying data across SAP and non-SAP sources into a single, governed, semantically consistent layer so that AI operates on facts that agree with each other. Neither is a product you activate and forget. Both are disciplines as much as technologies. But together they are the architecture that turns a fractured foundation into one that AI can stand on.

Clean Core is the principle of keeping the standard digital core of SAP S/4HANA clean, with customizations and extensions built on SAP Business Technology Platform rather than embedded into the core itself. The practical effect is that the core stays standard, which means it can be upgraded continuously, governed consistently, and understood by AI without having to account for thousands of undocumented local modifications. Custom capability does not disappear. It moves to a layer designed to hold it, where it can evolve independently of the core. An organization running a clean core is an organization whose systems behave the way the AI expects them to behave, which is the precondition for trusting the AI to act within them.

SAP Business Data Cloud, which this series covered in Issue 008, is the unified data foundation. It brings SAP and non-SAP data into a single governed layer with a common semantic meaning, so that a customer is the same customer everywhere, a transaction means the same thing in every system, and the data an AI operates on is consistent, traceable, and trusted. Its knowledge graph gives AI the business context to understand not just what the data says but what it means in the specific operating reality of the organization. This is the difference between an AI that processes data and an AI that understands a business. The foundation is what provides the understanding.

Finding Three — What
03
What Changes When the Foundation Is Finally Strong Enough to Build On

The payoff from a strong foundation is not a single dramatic outcome. It is a change in the economics and reliability of everything the organization does with AI from that point forward. The first and most immediate change is that pilots start reaching production. The gap between the AI an organization experiments with and the AI it actually runs in production, which for most organizations is the central frustration of their AI program, narrows dramatically, because the reasons pilots failed to scale were foundation reasons, and the foundation is now sound. The ambition that was ahead of the production reality finally has a path to catch up.

The second change is trust. AI outputs built on a governed, traceable, semantically consistent foundation can be used in the decisions that matter, because they can be defended. In a regulated industry this is not a nice-to-have. It is the line between an AI capability that can touch a real process and one that is confined forever to low-stakes experiments. The third change is compounding economics. A strong foundation is a one-time investment that every subsequent AI capability draws on. The first use case pays to build the foundation. Every use case after that inherits it, which means each new AI capability is faster, cheaper, and more reliable to deploy than the one before. Organizations without the foundation pay the full cost of the data and governance work again with every new initiative. Organizations with it pay once and compound.

The Bottom Line
The Foundation Is Not the Unglamorous Part of the AI Story. It Is the Whole Story.

It is tempting to treat the foundation as the boring prerequisite that has to be endured before the exciting AI work can begin. That framing is exactly backwards. The foundation is not the part you get through on the way to the value. It is the part that determines whether there is any value at all. The organizations that understand this stop asking which AI model to buy and start asking whether their data, their core, and their governance can carry the AI they already have. That is a less exciting question. It is also the only one that reliably predicts which organizations will have AI in production two years from now.

For organizations running SAP, the foundation is not a theoretical aspiration. It is a defined architecture, Clean Core for the process and system layer and SAP Business Data Cloud for the data and context layer, available now and connected to capabilities most organizations already hold. The work is real, and it is mostly a matter of discipline rather than acquisition, but the path is clear. And the reward is not a single use case. It is a foundation that every future AI capability inherits, so that the organization compounds its advantage while its competitors keep rebuilding the same railway for every train.

📍 Watch List — Your Foundation Action Agenda
→ Measure how far your core has drifted from standard: Ask for an honest assessment of how heavily your digital core has been customized and how much of that customization could move to BTP under a Clean Core approach. The size of that gap is a direct measure of how much friction your core will create for AI, and closing it is prerequisite work for trustworthy autonomous agents operating inside your systems.
→ Inventory where the same fact lives in disagreement: Identify the handful of critical data entities, such as customer, product, or counterparty, that are defined differently across your systems. Those disagreements are where AI outputs become unreliable. Unifying the highest-value entities first through Business Data Cloud is usually the fastest path from a fractured data estate to one AI can trust.
→ Test whether your AI outputs could survive an audit: Take one AI-assisted output your organization produces and ask whether you could trace it back to its source data, the controls it ran under, and the lineage that produced it. If you cannot, you have a governance gap that will confine AI to low-stakes use until it is closed. In a regulated industry, this is the test that decides what AI is allowed to touch.
→ Map your AI roadmap onto your foundation readiness: For each AI capability on your roadmap, ask what foundation it depends on and whether that foundation exists yet. The capabilities whose foundation is already sound are the ones to pursue first. The capabilities whose foundation is missing are not blocked forever, but they are telling you exactly what foundation work to prioritize now so they are not blocked later.
